The Unexpected Origin

There is a particular kind of person who walks into a room they were never supposed to enter and, by the time they leave, has made it theirs. Yuliana Grasman — known to the VRChat world simply as Afra — is that kind of person. The founder of Suika League didn't set out to build a gaming community. She wasn't even a gamer. She was a professional drifter.

Grasman became something of a quiet record-setter long before she ever touched a watermelon-puzzle game. She is the first woman in the world to perform freestyle drift with a 4x4 vehicle. She drove on two wheels in a category where, previously, no woman had. She raced in the UAE, competed in mudcross festivals, and won gold. The instinct that drew her to competitive gaming wasn't new — it was simply redirected.

"I've always gravitated toward things people said couldn't be done," Grasman says. "Not because I wanted to prove something to anyone in particular. Because I genuinely wanted to see what was on the other side of the barrier."

"A healthy competitive community shouldn't protect the people at the top. It should constantly create new people capable of reaching them."
— Afra (Yuliana Grasman), Founder, Suika League

VRChat found her the way many things find restless people: sideways. She had been exploring virtual reality as an extension of the performance work she had been doing on screen — acting, modeling, appearing alongside names like Shah Rukh Khan in Bollywood productions, and taking roles in the Emirati film circuit that cast her across genres and languages.

Virtual reality offered something cinema didn't: immediacy. There were no directors, no scripts, no waiting for the light to change. There was just a space and whoever showed up in it. For Grasman, that was enough to keep her coming back. The specific game — Suika, a deceptively simple fruit-merging puzzle with an iron grip on anyone who takes it seriously — caught her the same way competitive drifting had. Quietly, then completely.

Building Something Worth Losing At

The idea for Suika League didn't arrive as a business plan or a structured proposal. It arrived as a frustration. Grasman had been playing Suika Game in Melon & Chill — the VRChat world that serves as the game's social home — and noticed something that bothered her. The competitive layer was missing.

People played casually. They gathered, they merged fruit, they left. There were no records being kept, no rankings, no incentive to push beyond what was comfortable. For someone who had spent her career at the intersection of discipline and competition, this felt like a missed opportunity.

"Games are at their best when they make you want to get better," she explains. "Not better than someone else necessarily — better than the version of yourself who sat down five minutes ago. That's what competition gives you. That's what I wanted to give Suika."

"I wanted somebody to beat me. Not because I wanted to lose. Because I wanted the level around me to rise."
— Afra (Yuliana Grasman)

She began organizing. Keeping track of scores. Sharing results. Inviting people to try. The community formed organically around the structure she created, not the other way around. Within a relatively short period, the Discord had players, the leaderboard had names, and the name Suika League had come to mean something specific: this was where the serious players went.

What distinguished her approach from the beginning was the role system. Rather than treating all players as equal participants in an undifferentiated mass, Suika League assigned roles that reflected how people actually engaged. The Champion, the Competitive, the Sensei, the Host, the New Player — a taxonomy that acknowledged that communities are ecosystems, not populations, and that each type of member adds something the others can't.

"A Sensei isn't just a good player. A Sensei is someone the community can learn from," Grasman notes. "That distinction matters. If you're only measuring scores, you miss all the invisible work that makes a community actually function."

The tournaments followed. Then the magazine. Then the formal recognition of a leaderboard that tracked not just current scores but the arc of improvement. Each addition reflected the same underlying philosophy: that the things worth doing in a community are worth documenting, and that the people who show up deserve more than anonymity.

What It Became

Suika League grew. Players from different countries found their way to the Discord, to the leaderboard, to the weekly gatherings in Melon & Chill. People who had never met in person and likely never would found themselves genuinely invested in each other's progress. The #1 ranking changed hands. Scores that once seemed unreachable became targets.

What Grasman built is, in its own way, a proof of concept. The concept being: you can take something small — a fruit puzzle game on a VRChat stage — and build a structure around it serious enough that people rise to it. You don't need a massive budget or corporate backing. You need someone willing to keep showing up and keep the records.

She has done both. And she has done it while maintaining the other threads of her life — the performance work, the racing history, the personality that doesn't settle into any single category. This, too, seems intentional.

"The community didn't disappear because its founder wasn't standing in the middle of it. That's how you know it's real."
— Afra (Yuliana Grasman)

"I don't want Suika League to be about me," she says plainly. "I want it to be about the players who show up every week and put in the work. My job is to make sure the structure is there when they arrive."

The structure is there. The players keep arriving. And the scores — week by week, session by session — keep going up. Which is, in the end, exactly the point.
